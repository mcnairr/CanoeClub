Giving back to the river

We leave streams better than we found them

Conservation was written into the club’s purpose in 1956. Two traditions carry it today.

1960Every year since: 67 and counting

The annual river clean-up

A float trip with a purpose.

Once a year the club picks a stretch of Ozark river, camps on it for a weekend, and floats it slowly with empty canoes that come out full. Tires, coolers, cans, lawn chairs, the occasional refrigerator: if it does not belong in the river, it comes out in a boat. The tradition began in 1960, made the Associated Press wire in 1970 with a photo of the haul from the Current River, and has not missed a year.

It is also the best weekend of the year to meet the club. Everyone comes, the work is shared, and the evening around camp is earned.

Missouri Stream Team #41

Watching over the Blue River

When Missouri created its volunteer Stream Team program, OWWC signed up early, becoming Stream Team #41 on June 13, 1989. The club adopted the Blue River, which rises in Johnson County, Kansas, and winds through Swope Park and the east side of Kansas City on its way to the Missouri.

Four times a year, volunteers trained through the program meet at the Minor Park tennis courts (11477 Blue River Road) to test the Blue’s water quality, keeping an eye on the stream through the seasons and reporting what they find to the state. The next testing day is Saturday, January 9, at 10 a.m.

The club also turns out for community events on the river, like Blue River Discovery Days hosted by the Heartland Conservation Alliance, and members have staffed educational displays at schools, fairs, and events for years.

Want to help? Stream Team training is free and open to anyone. Come to a testing day or ask at a meeting and a member will get you started.

A longer view

Speaking up for wild rivers

The club’s founder helped start American Whitewater and argued for the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act. Members worked with the Ozark Society to save the Buffalo River from damming. That tradition continues in quieter ways: showing up for public comment, supporting the groups that protect the Ozarks, and teaching new paddlers to leave no trace.
